      Behind the design: decisions, learning science and build plan

      Design decisions

      Consequences, not "correct!" Feedback shows what happens next at work. People remember a colleague's reaction far better than a red cross.

      Three hidden scores. Each choice moves data safety, accuracy or ownership. Learners only see them at the end, so they choose the way they would at work instead of hunting for points.

      An ICAP lens on every choice. Each option is tagged by how much thinking it asks of the learner, from passive to interactive. This comes straight from my thesis research.

      No traps, no timer. The tempting options are things real people do under time pressure. Everything works with a keyboard.

      What I'd test in a pilot

      My biggest worry is that the best option in each scene looks too obvious. In a pilot with five or six people from the target group, I'd watch them think aloud and make the weaker options more tempting where needed.

      I'd also track which first choice people make. If most of them paste client data on the first try, that tells the business something its AI policy isn't getting across.

      that data is a gift to the business

      The learning science behind it

      First Principles of InstructionMerrill, 2002

      The module starts with a real work problem. Learners act first, see the result, and then apply what they noticed in the prompt task.

      Situated learningLave & Wenger, 1991

      Everything happens inside one realistic afternoon, with a real deadline and a colleague who reacts. The context is part of what people learn.

      Cognitive load theorySweller, 1988

      One decision per screen, three options, short text. Nothing on screen that doesn't help the decision.

      Feedback that moves learning forwardHattie & Timperley, 2007

      Each consequence answers "how am I doing?", and the results screen answers "where to next?" with a specific next step.

      ICAP frameworkChi & Wylie, 2014

      Every option is tagged by the engagement it asks for, and the prompt task is deliberately constructive: learners produce something new.

      Generation effectSlamecka & Graf, 1978

      Learners write their own prompt before they see a model answer. Producing it themselves makes it stick.

      Self-regulated learningZimmerman, 2002

      The "thinking path" at the end helps learners look back at their own strategy, which is the reflection phase of self-regulation.

      Project 2 · Research · Master's thesis

      AI and student engagement

      Master's thesis: AI-Supported Learning and ICAP-Based Engagement in Higher EducationUniversity of Augsburg
      In progress. Submission in December 2026; I'll add the results here.

      The question behind it

      Students now use AI before, during and after class. I'm less interested in whether they use it than in how. Does it help them think harder, or does it let them skip the thinking? That difference matters for every university and company now writing AI guidelines.

      I look at this through the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014). It sorts learning activities by how the learner engages: passive, active, constructive or interactive. Learning tends to get deeper as you move up that ladder.

      ICAP in short

      • Passive receiving: reading, listening
      • Active doing something with it: highlighting, copying, repeating
      • Constructive producing new ideas: explaining, comparing, questioning
      • Interactive building ideas together: debating, explaining to each other

      My research model

      The relationships I'm testing, and which research question looks at each one.

      1,743

      students in the original survey

      Bavaria

      several universities and disciplines, so student groups can be compared

      Survey

      quantitative design, secondary analysis of existing data

      Research questions

      • RQ1How do different purposes of AI use relate to students' engagement in digital learning activities?
      • RQ2To what extent is AI use associated with passive, active, constructive and interactive learning activities?
      • RQ3To what extent is satisfaction with digital equipment and infrastructure associated with more constructive and interactive learning activities?
      • RQ4How is AI use during courses, and during preparation and follow-up, associated with interactive and constructive technology-supported activities?
      • RQ5How do students' AI literacy and attitudes toward AI relate to their use of AI?

      What I measure

      • AI use: purposes, and timing in and around courses
      • AI literacy
      • Attitudes toward AI
      • Satisfaction with digital equipment and infrastructure
      • ICAP-based learning activities

      Working with an existing dataset means a lot of the work is careful: choosing variables and checking that each measure really fits the question I ask of it.

      Theories I build on

      ICAP frameworkChi & Wylie, 2014

      My main lens. It lets me describe engagement by what students actually do, not by how much time they spend.

      Technology Acceptance ModelDavis, 1989

      Helps explain why attitudes and perceived usefulness might shape whether and how students use AI (RQ5).

      AI literacyLong & Magerko, 2020

      What students need to know to use AI critically: what it can and can't do, and how to judge its output.

      Self-regulated learningZimmerman, 2002

      AI use in preparation and follow-up is part of how students plan, monitor and reflect on their learning (RQ4).

      From research to design

      The thesis is research, but I keep asking what it means for people who design learning. If the purpose of AI use matters more than how often people use it, then AI training should teach ways of using it, not just the tool. Here is how I already use that idea: the same task, with AI used at each ICAP level.

      This ladder is my own illustration of ICAP, not a thesis result. Whether a conversation with AI counts as "interactive" is still debated in research, which is part of why I find the topic so interesting.

        The theories behind the design

        The agenda follows Kolb's experiential learning cycle (1984). People experience something before I explain it, and they leave with an experiment to try.

        ExperienceVote on two product pages
        ReflectWhy did one sell better?
        ConceptualiseShort input on biases and colour
        ExperimentRole-play, then a one-week test at work
        AndragogyKnowles, 1984

        Adults need to know why something matters and want to use their own experience. Every activity starts from situations the team knows from the shop floor, which also helps with the "persuasion is pushy" resistance.

        Dual-process theoryKahneman, 2011

        The content of Module 1: fast, intuitive thinking and slow, deliberate thinking, and why most buying moments are fast.

        Social learning theoryBandura, 1977

        Role-plays and peer feedback let people watch each other try new pitches, which builds confidence faster than watching me.

        Transfer of trainingBaldwin & Ford, 1988

        Learning only matters if it reaches the job. The action plan and the team lead's 30-day check-in are there to support that transfer.
